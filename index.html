01
Fall 2025 · Scottish Highlands, Scotland

Architecture and Ecology

This project explores the tension between the vernacular house and the landscape it inhabits, using form, material, and topography to blur the boundary between the two. From the north elevation, the house presents itself as a familiar vernacular object, defined by a pitched roof, clapboard façade, and shingled roof. As the building moves east and south, this language is progressively disrupted: the geometry becomes organic, circular openings puncture the façade, and the roof dissolves into the terrain as a continuous green landscape.

Programmatically, the house follows the natural contours of the site, with interior spaces embedded within and shaped by the topography. Retaining walls carve into the ground to create an inner garden, allowing light and vegetation to penetrate the partially submerged master suite. Material shifts—from traditional shingles to reflective surfaces—further dissolve the architecture into its surroundings. Ultimately, the project redefines the relationship between interior and exterior, vernacular form and natural landscape, positioning the house as a mediator between human-made and nature-made systems.

Spring 2024 · Governors Island, New York City

Y-Wave

I engaged in an intensive exploration of ecological coastal armoring and urban resilience, culminating in the design and fabrication of innovative sea wall panels and intertidal ecological units for Governors Island, NYC. This project required a multidisciplinary approach, integrating urban ecology, material science, and advanced fabrication techniques. Working closely with industry partner Fort Miller Group, I developed and refined prototypes through form-finding experiments, digital fabrication, and full-scale casting, ensuring that my designs not only performed structurally but also fostered biodiversity along New York’s shoreline.

Expanding beyond ecological restoration, I translated these forms into sculptural interventions for public space and further investigated their architectural potential at an urban scale, testing how these geometries could inform facade systems and spatial articulation. This studio challenged me to navigate the intersections of performance, aesthetics, and adaptability, pushing my ability to design across scales while engaging with real-world environmental imperatives.

03
Spring 2026 · Mali, West Africa

Biotechnical Ruins

Evolutionary Ritual and the Living Machine

This thesis operates on two interconnected levels: one explicit and one underlying. The explicit component centers on a narrative that explores the intersection of electronic dance music and West African ritual traditions, positioning rhythm as a spatial, cultural, and communicative force through the lens of seven biotechnical ruins. The project highlights geopolitical and environmental conflicts in West Africa, specifically Mali, examining the impacts of external influence, including forced separation, resource extraction, and social and ecological disruption.

The underlying component offers a critique of architectural representation. It argues that imagery, narrative, and speculative design can be more effective than traditional drafting techniques in communicating ideas and shaping change. The thesis proposes that architectural thought could move beyond conventional representation and instead function as a responsive medium, and that architecture should be a medium for pause and reflection, where spaces are designed as active resources rather than static monumental entities.

“What happens when something like music becomes the reason for a landscape to shape and take form, or the value of music as a design method in which landscape is brought into being?”

Jeremy Kamal — designer, SCI-Arc and Harvard GSD Where Kamal asks this of landscape, this thesis takes up the question in architecture.
My Method of Design

My design methodology begins with a long-standing engagement with music and music theory. Through my experience, I came to understand architectural design as a process of layering, combining, and remixing ideas, such as DJing and sampling. I studied precedents in West African art, which utilize geometric abstraction to convey symbolic and cultural meaning. These forms informed a series of sketches and through hybridization, I combined these geometries to develop a cohesive architectural language. This emerging graphic standard became a framework for translating two-dimensional studies into three-dimensional forms. I then modeled these geometries and iteratively refined them, exploring how they could move, align, and situate themselves within the terrain and broader context of the site and narrative.

I drew from a range of influences, including Dune, Avatar, Pokémon, and Attack on Titan, as well as DJs such as Black Coffee, Carl Cox, and Osunlade. I also looked to architects and designers like Gary Polk, Jack Wasilewski, Jeremy Kamal, and Yiyu Zhou, alongside contemporary artists including Jean-Michel Basquiat, Tania Saggese, and Selome Muleta. The process finalized in investigating the program, allowing each object to shape space in ways that reinforce the central ideas of the thesis.

Living Machines

Seven ancient traveling machines. Living organisms. Titans that move across the landscape and emerge from hiding when the land needs them most. They do not serve people, but rather the land; if communities benefit from their existence, it is incidental.

01

Dusukun (Heart)

Its primary purpose is unknown. Metallic pillars stabilize it within the sand, while a red central core generates energy and emits sound through circular cavities in the surrounding interior structure.

02

Ji Fara (Filter)

It is a walking entity that observes and records the surrounding ecology.

03

Sanji Ka So (House of Rain)

Its primary purpose is unknown. It moves slowly like a desert beetle, its internal organs producing rhythmic sounds that draw people to dance. Beneath its tough exterior shell lies a series of water pools, dance chambers, and spaces for gathering and habitation.

04

Fanga Ponpe (Power)

It shapes the dunes as it moves and is believed to influence natural resources, even carrying rare earth minerals. Sought after by both foreign and local entities, it remains highly defensive, becoming aggressive only when the land is threatened.

05

Bolo Bolo (Hands)

This walking titan serves as a traveling archive, containing the historical knowledge of Mali’s indigenous peoples. It is entirely alive and non-inhabitable, with hybrid organic and technological tissues that amplify sound through speaker-like cavities along its body.

06

Sisi Wulilen (Mist Rising)

Its primary purpose is unknown. This stationary titan contains a central core that produces energy and sound, with vibrations traveling through the sand and felt beneath the feet of visitors. Elders regard it as a sacred listening chamber between land, rhythm, and memory.

07

Komzsi (Compressor)

Its purpose is unknown. This stationary titan remains embedded within the rocky desert mountains. People gather to experience its pools of water and listen to the sounds that echo through its chambers.

Fall 2023 · Rome, Italy

Shrouds and Thresholds

The Civic Center situated in Piazza del Parlamento, Rome, Italy, emerges as a deliberate response to the formidable challenges posed by the city’s unwavering commitment to historical preservation in the context of a rapidly evolving contemporary world. This architectural endeavor critically engages with the site’s historical legacy, offering a nuanced reflection on the physical history of Rome. In alignment with the broader narrative of major Western cities, Rome grapples with multifaceted 21st-century challenges.

The conceptualization and design of this Civic Center were strategically conceived to confront and navigate these contemporary issues while concurrently reclaiming urban space for the citizens of Rome. It is noteworthy that the prevailing landscape of Rome, both spatially and demographically, is heavily influenced by tourism. In response, the Civic Center serves as a pivotal embodiment of a communal space tailored to the needs of the Roman populace, acting as a sanctuary amidst the pervasive touristic influx. Through thoughtful design, this architectural intervention seeks to establish a harmonious balance between historical preservation, urban functionality, and the reclamation of public space for the residents of Rome.

05
Spring 2025 · Chelsea, New York City

Core Separation

This project was the second phase in a year-long sequence focused on the integration of architectural design with building systems, regulatory requirements, and material resolution. I developed a civic-scale architectural proposal for a contemporary branch library in Chelsea, New York City attached to The High Line. This library shares a concrete core central to the building. The core is separated from the rest of the building, but connected structurally through bridge points that the users can walk across to access the program within the core. The concrete core inhabits structural arches allowing for the program to be carved out of the core while still holding natural strength.

This project required a synthesis of site specific research, zoning and regulatory compliance, programmatic design, and environmental performance to create a meaningful public space that redefines the role of the library in the 21st century. Situated next to the High Line, my design responded to its unique urban context, balancing public accessibility with architectural innovation.

Fall 2025 · Blue Mountains, Jamaica

Yellow House

This project was conducted as an undergraduate research collaboration under Professor Titus, who selected me to assist in the development and refinement of a personal architectural investigation. Over the course of the semester, I worked closely with him to help conceptualize the project’s spatial and material intentions and to translate these ideas into a coherent, buildable residential proposal.

The house is conceived as a retreat for painting and reading, defined by precise geometries, sharp intersections, and layered spatial relationships. The lower level is organized as two distinct volumes separated by an open central space, while the upper level unifies the structure through a carved courtyard that punctures the mass of the roof, connecting interior and exterior conditions. This vertical void links the floors both visually and experientially, while a bridging element, understood as an elevated garden, extends the second level outward, reinforcing the relationship between landscape, light, and inhabitation.

Spring 2022 · Troy, New York

The Eco-Pavilion

Along the waterfront of the Hudson River in Troy, New York, lies the eco pavilion that defies conventions and embodies the future of sustainable design. This hypothetical structure, deeply rooted in the language of voxels, offers a transformative interpretation of scale, clarity, and pixelization. This deliberate integration of flora amplifies the pavilion’s ecological attributes, creating a harmonious coexistence between architecture and nature. The pavilion’s overwhelming presence becomes a testament to its commitment to ecological assistance and contribution.

The pavilion serves as a purifier, actively contributing to the health of marine life and providing a solution for the purification of water, ensuring the well-being of both the aquatic ecosystem and the local community. This visionary pavilion transcends its initial role, becoming a symbol of regenerative design that seamlessly integrates technology, architecture, and nature. It invites the people of Troy to witness the powerful union of human innovation and environmental sensitivity—a pavilion that not only opens its doors for leisure and protection but also actively participates in the rejuvenation and sustainability of the local ecosystem.

09
Fall 2025 · Blue Mountains, Jamaica

L-House

This project was an undergraduate research collaboration with Professor Titus, focusing on the investigation of geometric systems as generators of spatial and experiential qualities in domestic architecture. The house is structured around a repeated L-shaped geometry, with L-forms stacked, shifted, and layered to produce moments of overlap, enclosure, and release. This formal strategy allows the architecture to maintain a disciplined geometric clarity while generating complex spatial relationships.

At the center of the composition, a double-height courtyard is carved from the intersecting L-forms, establishing a vertical void that visually and spatially connects the first and second floors. The courtyard functions as the primary organizational element of the house, drawing light deep into the interior, facilitating natural ventilation, and mediating the relationship between interior spaces and the surrounding landscape. Programmatically, the stacked geometry enables a clear separation between spaces for reading and painting while maintaining visual continuity across levels.

10
Spring 2023 · Troy, New York

Multi Unit Collective Housing

In response to the evolving challenges of urban living, this project reimagines housing in the heart of downtown Troy, focusing specifically on multi-unit collective housing. In a world where aspects of our lives are increasingly shared in collective spheres, our domestic spaces often remain rooted in the ethos of individual property and standalone structures. Traditionally manifested in post-World War II suburban developments, this approach is unsustainable in the face of rapid urbanization and escalating demands on the Earth’s resources.

Embracing a global cultural necessity, this project envisions a mixed-use, multi-unit housing complex situated at the prominent intersection of Congress and Third Streets in downtown Troy, NY. Beyond mere architectural design, this undertaking is a conscious effort to materialize principles of exchange and communication within urban spaces. It seizes the opportunity to shape the city’s form and materiality into meaningful daily experiences, recognizing that the act of designing housing is inherently socio-political.

By skillfully navigating the practical challenges of organizing an architectural program, the project achieves a harmonious balance between meeting users’ needs and engaging in high levels of formal, material, and aesthetic experimentation. The result is a forward-thinking architectural solution that not only responds to its immediate context but also contributes to the broader discourse within the discipline.

View House

In this project, I was entrusted with the design of an extension for the Thomas Cole Museum in Catskill, New York. My endeavor involved crafting a view house meticulously engineered to offer visitors a superlative experience within an environment characterized by comfort and optimal circulation. The primary objective was to seamlessly integrate the exhibition of artworks and artifacts while strategically guiding visitors towards significant views afforded by the hilltop location of the Thomas Cole House.

The architectural intervention not only prioritized the curated display of artworks but also facilitated an immersive journey through the site’s natural and cultural landscape. This was achieved through a meticulous orchestration of circulation pathways designed to seamlessly integrate with the surrounding vistas, enhancing the overall narrative of the museum experience.

Moreover, the project was complemented by the integration of technical construction systems drawings, providing comprehensive insights into the materiality, structural integrity, and overall construction methodology. These technical specifications not only elucidated the intricacies of the built environment but also underscored a commitment to architectural excellence and meticulous attention to detail.

Selected Drawings

Sketches

Drawing has always been at the center of my creative identity. Before I began studying architecture, it served as a form of release and personal reflection. A way to express ideas and emotions that were often too abstract for words. Over time, and especially through the rigor of architectural education, my relationship with drawing has matured from that of simple artistic expression to a deeper understanding of it as a tool of inquiry, communication, and construction.

In architecture, drawing is not merely a representation of a finished idea but a medium through which ideas are discovered and tested. Every mark carries intention; every line holds information. Its length, tone, and thickness communicate dimension, structure, hierarchy, and atmosphere. The smallest gesture can suggest the scale of a building, the rhythm of a façade, or the intimacy of a space. Through this lens, drawing becomes both analytical and emotional—an intersection of precision and imagination that allows thought to materialize on paper.

For me, the act of drawing remains one of the most important stages of the design process. I believe that the first conception of architecture should emerge through pencil and paper. Only after this moment of tactile exploration do I move into digital media.

Throughout this section, I present a range of drawings. From quick sketches and technical studies to layered, painterly compositions. Together, they reveal how I approach design, space, and the built environment.

13
Fall 2023 · Rome, Italy

Fashion

During my semester in Rome, I enrolled in a course titled Fashion in Architecture, where I explored the intersection between architectural thinking and the language of fashion design. Through a series of material experiments in folding, crimping, and stitching fabrics, metals, and papers—I developed sculptural studies that tested structure, form, and tactility. These became conceptual “scenes” and palettes for speculative garments.

Drawing inspiration from architectural precedents, I analyzed the dialogue between linear and painterly forms in architecture and translated those spatial ideas into wearable compositions. I sketched multiple iterations of dresses and clothing lines that merged geometric form-finding with curated color palettes and textures.

This experimental and iterative process deepened my understanding of what it means to design. Not only as an architect but as a creator of identity and experience. Whether through a building that can be inhabited or a garment that can be worn, design operates as a medium for self-expression and cultural dialogue. Guided by a renowned fashion and costume designer recognized internationally for her cinematic work, the course revealed to me how profoundly fashion influences the way architecture engages with culture, material, and the human body.

Narrative

The narrative follows Omar, a Bambara DJ born and raised within the rocky and desert landscapes of Mali, West Africa. Rooted in Bambara beliefs, the religion emphasizes ancestral veneration, the presence of spirits within nature, and a collective ritual. Over time, he has traveled through European and U.S. electronic music powerhouses such as Spain, Germany, Chicago, and New York, DJ’ing and sharing his distinct style of Afro-politan house music. As he describes it, his sound is “home-brewed, but future-focused,” blending rhythms with contemporary electronic expression.

Before this, Omar grew up as a shepherd boy, tending to his family’s animals and developing an intimate relationship with the land. As he matured, he began to witness the geopolitical shifts shaping his homeland: increasing Western influence encroaching upon Mali, extracting vital resources while neglecting the cultural and ecological fabric of the region.

Throughout his childhood, his parents and grandparents told stories of the Mali Titans. These massive biotechnical ruins that emerge only when the land needs them most. These beings, they said, communicated through rhythm: through drums and sound. Ancestral Mali people throughout time have gathered in ritual to dance to the imitation of the sounds the titans created. Worshiping their strength, presence, and cultural intelligence and respect for the land. Omar came to understand his own music as an iteration of this, an evolutionary ritual to the living machines. So when word spread that the Titans had returned, Omar knew it was time to come home.

About

An architectural designer exploring the intersection of nature, history, and innovation in the built environment. My work seeks to integrate landscape and ecology into architecture while drawing inspiration from global traditions and local cultural contexts. By merging historical references with contemporary design strategies, I aim to create spaces that are functional, sustainable, and culturally resonant. I am particularly interested in how architecture can foster a dialogue between the natural and the constructed, and how materials, form, and site can work together to create environments that honor history while embracing the future. One of my goals is to design spaces that evoke meaning, support community, and embody a sense of place that is both timeless and forward-looking.
